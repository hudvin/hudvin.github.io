## A bit of lyricism

Thursday, September 9. I'm on edge. In 6 hours I need to go to Kyiv's Zhulyany airport. And I categorically do not want to go there. Basically nothing is ready - unclear what's with the knee, Poland's vaccination requirements are murky, the route is poorly calculated, part of the gear isn't checked or bought. All I want is to take the cat, the laptop, crawl under the blanket and spend the next two weeks watching TV shows.

Around 3 AM I call a taxi. For the tenth time I check if I've closed all the taps and turned off all the appliances. I take the heavy backpack, jump into the car, remember something, go back, check everything again and sit down again. About 15 minutes through the empty city and I'm standing in front of the airport entrance. At the entrance - checking for masks and (I think) certificates. I wrap the backpack in plastic. After about 30 minutes I check my bag, check in and go to the gate. Now yes, there's a feeling that something is beginning. Boarding and a short flight to Poland. At customs they don't ask anything about certificates. Even a bit annoying, considering how much time I spent on this paperwork crap. An hour and a half of waiting - and a short flight to Arlanda airport (near Stockholm). Mild freaking out from local prices (the most expensive cola and the most expensive burger of my life), a short walk around the city, buying maps and camping gas - and to the station. The start point is still 16 hours of travel to the north.

Ahead - a bit of suffering. Since I bought the tickets a week before departure, the choice was so-so. No spots left in the sleeper and for just 70 dollars I bought a ticket in a seated carriage. And.... Usually Ukraine buys various junk in Europe, somehow washes/cleans it and uses it for another 10-20 years. This carriage kind of hinted that Ukraine somehow managed to level up this scheme and pawn off worn-out Ukrainian Railways carriages to Sweden. I find it hard to explain the existence of carriages in Sweden that are so-so even for Ukraine any other way.

So, Friday evening. Two days without sleep. The brain is no longer very picky about how to sleep. An eye mask and a sweater under the head are enough. The only thing in the way is a neighbor who is peeling tangerines slowly and with sadistic pleasure.

4 AM. A crimson dawn over the tundra, which still looks like Ukrainian nature - ordinary trees, birches and all that. Already yellow though.

Sleepy, I tumble out of the train at Murjek station.

Early morning, cold. The grass is covered in frost. A small station. An hour later a bus arrives that takes me and about 10 other people to Kvikkjokk. This is where I start.

## Why solo?

I just wanted to be alone for a bit.

First, from time to time I posted messages on my FB looking for companions. Responses over the whole time - 0. And I didn't want to go with just anyone anyway.

Second, few people can go for two and a half weeks.

Third, the logistics even for one person turned out to be intricate.

Fourth, I really wanted to be alone and gather my thoughts. And when you travel with someone, you're always more connected to your companions than to the surrounding world. And that gets in the way of looking around. And this time I really wanted to look at the world.

Feeling like something is broken there or a knife is stuck in. Every step hurt like hell. Over three years I visited many doctors. What didn't they prescribe me - massages, procedures, injections, pills, exercises. But the procedures only had a positive effect on the clinics' budgets. ## What? Where? When?

I found out about Kungsleden completely by accident about 3 years ago when I was trying to find something interesting around Europe. The Arctic North, endless tundra, rivers, lakes, hundreds of kilometers of routes, absence of people - all inclusive for someone who doesn't like crowds. For reasons of lack of either time, health, or money, the trip was postponed three years in a row. This year everything came together - managed to fix the knee, take vacation and update the gear. Decided - I'm going. Don't know exactly how, but I'm going.

Hung a large map of Scandinavia on the wall, took guidebooks from the bookshelf and dove into Google. Lots of transfers, unclear moments with boat crossings, camping gas, food and COVID certificates, attempts to fit the logistics into minimal time. Several times I thought about dropping the idea. But.... It all worked out!

### Sweden and tourism

Before this trip I knew exactly two facts about Sweden - one about bad weather, the other about high prices. Knew nothing about tourism and nature. Also heard that Swedes don't actually like the Karlsson-on-the-Roof character much.

Kungsleden - "the king's trail" translated from Swedish - the most famous route. Stretches for more than 400 km from Hemavan to Abisko. The route can be done both north to south (the more popular option) and south to north. Pros of the latter - the sun shines on your back (useful if you use a solar panel to charge gadgets), you avoid crowds (you pass each other since you're going opposite directions). Roughly half the route is above the Arctic Circle.



Overview map of Kungsleden:

<img src="/assets/kungsleden/photos/other/Kungsleden_map_overview.png" alt="Kungsleden_map_overview" style="zoom:50%;" />

The route is roughly split into three parts:

* northern - Abisko to Kvikkjokk - 216 km

* central - Kvikkjokk to Ammarnäs - 179 km

* southern - Ammarnäs to Hemavan - 79 km

In the northern and southern parts every 15-20 kilometers there are huts and you can go without a tent. Some huts have small shops. In Kvikkjokk and Saltoluokta there are full tourist stations with electricity, shower, shop, cafe etc. In the central part there are no huts and you can't manage without a tent.

You can get onto the route at several points - Abisko, Hemavan, Singi (via Kebnekaise), Kvikkjokk and a few others.

I got there by train to Murjek station, from there by bus to Kvikkjokk. From Abisko there's a direct train to Stockholm.

I'm doing the northern part + Kebnekaise + Tarfala Valley. About 240 km total.

Maps can be bought in Stockholm (see the link to the tourist shop below). For my route I needed 3 pieces (at 169 SEK / 20 USD each).

### Route variations

- from Tarfala Valley you can go through the glaciers and return to the main route (instead of going back via Kebnekaise)

- from Singi you can go to Kebnekaise and then to Nikkaluokta. From there get to Kiruna airport.

- in season you can climb Kebnekaise

- from Kvikkjokk you can go into Sarek National Park
- routes around Abisko
- routes around Alesjaure

in Sweden there are a lot of STF huts (Swedish Tourist Association) and you can put together a route for any taste

### Weather

Tundra is usually damp, cold and windy. I got lucky. Rain only once, but the wind at night was sometimes very strong. On the second-to-last night it tore the tent. True, the tent was pitched a bit dumb - across the wind. While I tried to take it down - the hole got even bigger. In September it's already cold enough and around Tjäktja Pass standing water froze about two centimeters. Many streams froze completely.

One way or another gear needs to be chosen assuming it'll be cold, damp and windy. Good waterproof clothes and boots are mandatory.

### Wildlife

In summer there's a lot of midges. In September - no.

Bears live here, but it's hard to run into them. Attacks are extremely rare. No practical danger.

There are reindeer, skittish like kittens.

### A bit about tourist stations

Kungsleden is the most famous of the Swedish routes. But far from the only one. Across the country there are many tourist huts (https://www.swedishtouristassociation.com/discover/?facilities), you can pitch a tent almost everywhere, so you can make whatever route you want.

I almost always stayed out in the open. Wanted silence, nice views and solitude.

In simple huts there's usually a small shop (at the end of the season there's almost nothing left though), a kitchen (with a gas stove and cookware), a couple dozen sleeping spots, a toilet. Prices in the shop are 2-3 times higher than in the country. For example, freeze-dried meals that cost 7 dollars in Stockholm are sold in Saltoluokta for 15 dollars. But there was no choice :(

You can either sleep inside or pitch a tent nearby and use the hut facilities at certain hours. Cost - see the link above.



### How to get there

![](/assets/kungsleden/photos/s21/20210910_121558.jpg)

Attempts to figure out how to get to Kvikkjokk and return from Abisko took a lot of time. I could have flown out on Friday, but then I'd have been stuck in Murjek or Jokkmokk - no buses on weekends. On weekends you also can't buy camping gas - shops are closed. If you don't go to Murjek but fly to Gällivare, you can buy gas, but you won't get to the start of the route - no buses on weekends. While I was solving this puzzle, tickets for trains and planes started running out and getting more expensive at the same time. A week before moment X I moved the departure by a day (from Friday to Thursday), decided to buy camping gas in Stockholm, took a train ticket to Murjek (leaves at 5 PM from Stockholm, arrives early morning), a bus from Murjek to Kvikkjokk and a plane for the night from Thursday to Friday. The puzzle came together. Getting back from Abisko is simple - there's a direct train to Stockholm from there.

Getting to the station from Arlanda airport can be done by bus, regular train, or Arlanda Express (299 SEK / 35 USD). I used the last one. 20 min at 200 km/h - and you're at the station. Ticket can be bought at one of the many yellow machines.

As a rule I tried to walk a couple of kilometers past a hut (marked with "+" in the schedule below) and pitch the tent around 7 PM. If there's a crossing - you need to make it by 4 or 5 PM. And the main thing - the overnight spot should be beautiful ).

### Alesjaure--  -  Alesjaure  -  Abiskojaure

Looks like bad luck found me again!

Wake up from the noise of wind and the tent flapping in my face. Not yet 6 AM. Lie for about 15 minutes. Pull the earplugs out of my ears and it immediately gets very loud. Realize that outside - some kind of hell. Very strong wind. Slightly open the tent. Whoa! The outer layer started tearing. Near the loop where the stake goes in, there's a rip of about 20 centimeters. Quickly put on boots and jump outside. Wind is very strong. Try to pin the tent edges with rocks and the backpack. They don't hold. The rip gets bigger and bigger. Eventually pull out the front pole and throw the backpack on the whole back part. The rip is already about a meter. The tent is done for. Angry, I pack things in emergency mode. Being an idiot - pull out the side stakes but leave the central pole and the end stakes. Holding the pole, I can't reach the stakes. The wind makes the rip even bigger. Basically 2/3 of the back of the outer fly is torn off.

Somehow shove the tent into the backpack.

Angry.

Two more days to Abisko. Where to sleep - unclear. Vaguely remember that at each station they usually leave an emergency room open. A torn tent is very much an emergency case.

SD card lost, tent torn, where to sleep - dunno. Vadym, wanted adventures? Here you go!

Soon I reach Alesjaure. Sit on the threshold of some shed, finally make breakfast, drink tea. Think about this and that.

And....

Ah, to hell with it!

I'm in Sweden, on a trek I dreamed about for three years! About 70% of the photos survived, and there are some shots from the phone. And in the worst case I'll sleep on the threshold of some hut. I have a warm sleeping pad and a sleeping bag. From the tent I can cobble together something like a tarp. I'll survive wind and cold. No frost or heavy rain expected. Only one overnight left. In the worst case I'll be a bit wet and tired.  

Cheered up, I go on. Pass the most beautiful lakes surrounded by mountain peaks. Pity I have to walk so fast. Sometimes you want to sit by every little rock and admire the landscapes.

Stop by a small shelter before a crossing (luckily I don't need to boat anywhere), study the map. Relatively nearby should be a shelter where I can have lunch. Reach it in another hour and a half.

## What to take

### Gear

- camping gas bought in Stockholm. Usually you can buy it on the route, but this year the supplier had some problems.
- maps - the internet is full of track recordings. Google Kungsleden GPS. For navigation I used Garmin InReach + osmand + maps.cz
- for communication I used the Garmin InReach satellite messenger. It lets you receive and send sms and email.
- photography - Samsung S21 and Sony A6500. About the latter - a separate story below.
- sleeping bag Marmot Helium, comfort rating -9°C
- tent Zephyros 2 Lite. Was torn by wind on the second-to-last day of the hike. In the tent's defense - it's about 7 years old and I pitched it very dumb - perpendicular to the wind.
- other clothes - down jacket, fleece, shell jacket, rain pants (used once), base layer, hat, gloves
- boots Bestard Breithorn pro
- Anker solar panel
- usb light
- Jetboil
- backpack Osprey Xenith 88. Weighed about 21 kg on the route (lots of food). A bit big and heavy by itself.
- headlamp BD Storm. Good, but need something simpler. 350 lumens is a big electricity drain, and I don't need the red-blue-green light show for free.
- pad Therm-a-Rest Neoair XLite. Very warm. No, didn't puncture it )
- powerbank 20000
- knee braces - didn't use

Overall everything was more or less fine. Except the boots should have been broken in.

### Food

Part bought in Kyiv, part on the route (mostly in Saltoluokta). In Kyiv took freeze-dried meals, Adventure Food (more expensive, but much higher calorie and tastier), in Sweden bought the same Adventure Food (just 15 dollars a pack), dry noodles like Cup Noodles and canned soups. In season food can be bought without problems at any station. But I went at the end of the season and the assortment was limited to soups and beans. How I sometimes dreamed of pizza!))

Water is great - you can take it from any stream, river or lake.

### Gas

As a rule camping gas can be bought on the route in the huts. But this year the supplier had some problems and it had to be bought in Stockholm. Took 2 canisters of 230g (at 79 SEK / 9 USD) + 1 of 100g (69 SEK / 8 USD). At the end of the hike in the last canister (230g) there was about 30% left. So I think the 100g one could have been skipped.

### Photography and video

Took Samsung S21 and Sony A6500 with a couple of lenses - Sony 16-70mm, f/4 OSS Carl Zeiss and Sigma 30mm f/1.4 DC DN Contemporary Lens for Sony.

And this was the biggest and perhaps only mistake.

This is not a camera. This is a piece of shit.

The lens (for just 1000 dollars) - after 3 days the springs in the cap cracked. Some genius decided to make them out of plastic. No weather sealing against moisture and dust. This lens spent almost the whole route in the backpack. Used the Sigma instead. The battery drained instantly even in warm weather. Sometimes it seemed like it discharges even if you just put it next to the camera. Had to constantly pull out the battery and put it in a pocket. At some point the card with 3 days of photos flew out and got lost. Three batteries barely lasted 1 day. Constantly had to think about charging with the solar panel. Sometimes the USB port in the camera came loose. Every day I wanted to yell at it across the whole Arctic.

For comparison - Canon 6D. Over 5 years it lay in dirt, sand, snow, fell on rocks, a couple of times I broke a fall with it against snow and ice. And nothing! Only the paint peeled a bit. 3 batteries lasted a week. Worked even in a hard frost. You could damn well hammer nails with it.

Conclusion for myself is simple - Sony can only be used for the city. For hikes - out of the question. Next time I plan to take either the good old 6d, or some Canon R.

Video I shot on a freshly acquired S21.
